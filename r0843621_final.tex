\section{Final design of the evolutionary algorithm \hfill(target: $3.5$ pages)} 

\subsection{The three main features}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Mixed population initialization (greedy + random).}
  A small fraction of the initial population is seeded via multi-start nearest-neighbour greedy tours, with the remainder sampled uniformly at random.

  \item \textbf{Deterministic crowding with edge-based similarity.}
  Survivor selection is diversity-preserving: each offspring competes only against its closest rival under directed edge overlap (successor representation), preventing rapid population collapse.

  \item \textbf{Memetic refinement via Or-opt local search.}
  Or-opt is integrated as a periodic refinement step (applied after initialization and then every $N$ generations to top-$k$ individuals), providing strong improvement, especially on sparse large instances.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Representation}\label{sec_rep}

Candidates are represented as a \textbf{permutation} of city indices, i.e., a tour
$\pi=(\pi_1,\dots,\pi_n)$ visiting each city exactly once; fitness is the total cycle length including the return
edge to $\pi_1$. This encoding is natural for TSP and works directly with permutation-aware operators (OX,
inversion, insertion), while enabling efficient evaluation and local modifications (e.g., Or-opt).

Tours are stored as \texttt{numpy.ndarray} integers (\texttt{Individual.tour}) for fast slicing/reversal and
Numba-accelerated evaluation. For deterministic crowding, a temporary successor representation
($\mathrm{succ}[i]=$ next city) is derived from the permutation to compute directed edge overlap.


\subsection{Initialization}

The initial population is created using a \textbf{mixed strategy}: a small fraction
($\texttt{GREEDY\_FRACTION}\cdot \lambda$) is generated with a \textbf{nearest-neighbour greedy} heuristic
(multi-start from random cities), and the remaining individuals are \textbf{uniformly random permutations}.
Candidates are evaluated immediately; with \texttt{USE\_PENALTY\_FOR\_INF} enabled, tours containing missing
edges receive a large finite penalty ($\texttt{INF\_PENALTY\_FACTOR}=10.0$) rather than being discarded.

The population size is fixed to $\lambda=100$ as a time-budget trade-off: larger populations reduce the number
of generations (and thus crossover events), while smaller populations lose diversity and converge faster. The
greedy seed is intentionally small (default $\texttt{GREEDY\_FRACTION}=0.01$, i.e., 1 individual for
$\lambda=100$) to provide high-quality building blocks without taking over the population; over generations,
crossover propagates useful edges into the predominantly random population.

\subsection{Selection operators}

Parent selection uses \textbf{$k$-tournament selection}: for each parent, $k$ individuals are sampled uniformly
at random from the population and the individual with the lowest fitness is selected. This choice is simple,
efficient, and provides a direct handle on selection pressure.

The main parameter is $\texttt{TOURNAMENT\_K}=7$. This increases early
exploitation by selecting high-quality individuals (including the greedy seed) more frequently as parents, but
excessive values risk premature convergence; therefore $\texttt{TOURNAMENT\_K}$ is kept constant (no schedule).


\subsection{Mutation operators}

A \textbf{mutation portfolio} is used for permutation tours: \texttt{swap} (exchange two cities),
\texttt{inversion} (reverse a segment), \texttt{insertion} (move one city), and \texttt{scramble} (randomly
permute a segment). When mutation is applied, the operator is sampled by a weighted roulette with probabilities
0.20, 0.60, 0.15, and 0.05 respectively.

Randomness is controlled by an \textbf{individual-specific mutation rate} $\alpha$: mutation is applied with
probability $\alpha$, with $\alpha \sim U[\alpha_{\min},\alpha_{\max}]$ at initialization and inherited by
offspring (self-adaptive). No generation-dependent schedule is used.

The portfolio increases population-level exploration; mutation rarely improves the elite directly, but it helps
maintain diversity and introduces new building blocks for crossover.



\subsection{Recombination operators}

Recombination uses \textbf{Order Crossover (OX)} for permutation tours. OX copies a random contiguous segment
from parent 1 into the offspring and fills the remaining positions by scanning parent 2 in order, inserting the
cities that are not yet present. This preserves a block from parent 1 while retaining relative order information
from parent 2; by construction each city appears exactly once, so the offspring is a valid permutation.

Crossover is applied with probability $\texttt{CROSSOVER\_PROB}=1.0$ to maximize mixing under the fixed time
budget. Segment boundaries are sampled uniformly at random, controlling disruption (short segments preserve more
of parent 2's order; long segments preserve more of parent 1's block). No self-adaptivity or schedule is used.

PMX and other permutation crossovers were considered, but OX was retained due to its simplicity and robust
behavior for TSP-style order preservation in this implementation.


% elimination is critical!

\subsection{Elimination operators}

Survivor selection uses \textbf{deterministic crowding}. After offspring are generated, each child competes only
against its \emph{most similar} individual in the current population (its rival) and replaces it only if it has
better fitness. Similarity is measured by \textbf{directed edge overlap} using a successor representation
($\mathrm{succ}[i]$ = next city), i.e., the number of matching directed edges between two tours; ties are broken
using closest fitness.

This local competition preserves diversity by preventing genetically distant individuals from replacing each
other, which reduces premature convergence. A direct consequence is that the population mean can remain much
worse than the elite solution, since improvements mainly replace similar tours rather than pushing the entire
population toward the best.

Standard \text{($\mu+\lambda$) truncation} was tested earlier, but it increased selection pressure too strongly
and caused rapid population collapse, so deterministic crowding was retained in the final version.

\subsection{Local search operators}

An \textbf{Or-opt} local search is used. Or-opt removes a short consecutive chain of cities and reinserts it
elsewhere; improving moves are accepted and repeated for a limited number of iterations. The chain length is
fixed to $\ell \leq \texttt{OR\_OPT\_MAX\_CHAIN\_LEN}=3$.

Or-opt is computationally demanding, so it is accelerated with \textbf{Numba} and applied sparingly: once
immediately after initialization, and then every $\texttt{LS\_INTERVAL}=100$ generations to only the top
$\texttt{LS\_TOP\_K}=1$ individual(s). It is the main driver for beating the greedy heuristic on sparse instances
(750--1000 cities): without Or-opt, the GA rarely improves beyond the greedy baseline, whereas on dense instances
(50--500 cities) improvements are still common.


\subsection{Diversity promotion mechanisms}

Diversity is promoted via the \textbf{deterministic crowding} survivor selection described above. Similarity is
computed using directed edge overlap from a successor representation (via \texttt{build\_successor} and
\texttt{best\_rival\_index\_by\_edge\_overlap}), which is computationally demanding but limits replacement to
genetically similar tours and reduces premature convergence.

As a consequence, the best individual can stabilize quickly while the population mean improves more gradually;
no additional diversity control or adaptive scheme is used beyond crowding.


\subsection{Stopping criterion}

Termination follows the provided \textbf{time-based} criterion from the template: each generation calls
\texttt{Reporter.report(...)} and the run stops as soon as the reporter signals that the time limit is reached
(return value $<0$). No additional stopping conditions are used. Two internal safeguards,
$\texttt{GENERATIONS}=10{,}000{,}000$ and $\texttt{STALL\_LIMIT}=10{,}000{,}000$, are kept for testing when the
time limit is not enforced; in the final configuration they are set high enough not to trigger before the time
limit.


\subsection{Parameter selection}

Most parameters were chosen by iterative tuning based on observed performance (final objective and convergence
behavior) under the 5-minute budget. Candidate settings were evaluated across all provided instances, with
particular emphasis on the larger/sparser cases (750 and 1000 cities), since improvements that stabilize these
harder instances typically also carry over to the smaller/dense ones, whereas the reverse is not always true.

\subsection{Other considerations}\label{sec_oth}

No island-model, multiobjective optimization, or explicit parallelization was implemented; the focus was on
single-objective performance under the fixed time budget. The main optimization is \textbf{Numba} acceleration
for performance-critical routines executed repeatedly: fitness evaluation and the edge-overlap computations used
by deterministic crowding (edge-based diversity preservation). The greedy constructor is only executed during
initialization. Numba compiles selected Python functions to optimized machine code at runtime via LLVM, often
reaching near C speed.

\paragraph{Instance-dependent parameters.}
The only instance-dependent quantities are derived from the number of cities $n$ in the input distance matrix.
Each individual is a permutation of length $n$, and the sparsity detector computes the fraction of off-diagonal
$\infty$ entries over $n(n-1)$. The edge-diversity monitor depends on $n$ (and population size $|\mathcal{P}|$)
and normalizes the number of unique directed edges by
\[
\frac{\lvert E_{\text{unique}}\rvert}{\min(|\mathcal{P}|\,n,\; n(n-1))},
\]
since the number of observed unique edges cannot exceed the total edges present in the population
($|\mathcal{P}|\,n$) nor the total number of possible directed edges ($n(n-1)$).

\section{Critical reflection \hfill(target: $1.5$ page)}

\subsection{Strengths and weaknesses}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Initialization}

  Even a very small greedy seed (e.g., one individual) provides high-quality building blocks early, accelerating convergence toward short tours (including in ablations where local search is disabled); if a cheap greedy heuristic is available, it is worth using to avoid starting purely blind/random.

  \item \textbf{Diversity}

  Maintaining a population enables exploration of multiple regions of the search space in parallel; with edge-based deterministic crowding, genetic material is preserved and the population does not collapse into a single lineage.

  \item \textbf{Local search}

  Combining evolutionary search with Or-opt provides strong intensification near high-quality regions, allowing faster escape from local minima and substantial refinement once the search is close to a good basin.

\end{enumerate}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Sensitivity on sparse instances.}
  On sparse graphs (e.g., \texttt{tour1000}), improvements become rare and beating the greedy baseline reliably often requires strong local search (Or-opt), indicating a tendency to rely on intensification when the feasible search space is constrained.

  \item \textbf{Computational overhead of diversity control.}
  Edge-based deterministic crowding requires expensive similarity computations; Numba mitigates this, but a lighter diversity mechanism could reduce overhead and free budget for more generations/evaluations.

  \item \textbf{Stagnation and basin trapping.}
  Once a strong basin is found, the best solution can plateau for long periods, and escaping to a better optimum becomes unlikely without additional mechanisms.
\end{enumerate}

In practice, it can be difficult to attribute improvements to specific components (selection pressure, crossover,
mutation, local search, or elimination), since multiple mechanisms interact simultaneously. Detailed logging and
controlled experiments where one component is disabled at a time are therefore important to understand where
progress actually comes from. It is also easy to focus exclusively on the best individual; however, as this is
primarily a genetic-algorithm project, the population dynamics (diversity, mean fitness, and convergence
behavior) are an essential part of the evaluation.

Another challenge is parameter sensitivity: performance can change noticeably with small parameter adjustments
and depends on instance characteristics such as tour length and sparsity. Consequently, parameter choices should
be validated across multiple instance sizes, not only on the easiest cases.

Overall, evolutionary search is an appropriate approach for quickly producing solutions that improve upon cheap
heuristics (especially on dense graphs), while exact optimality is better targeted with specialized solvers.
Genetic algorithms remain valuable when strong problem-specific solvers are unavailable, when robustness across
instances matters, or when good solutions are needed under a strict time budget.

\subsection{GenAI peer reviewing critical reflection} 

The AI-generated review was generally well-written at the surface level (e.g., fewer spelling/grammar issues),
but it tended to remain generic and high-level, with limited concrete, context-specific critique compared to the
human review. This likely follows from limited domain understanding: plausible-sounding feedback is easy to
generate, while identifying subtle methodological issues requires strong technical grounding and careful reading.

Overall, the AI review was only mildly useful as a checklist-style assistant (structure, clarity, missing items),
but not as a reliable evaluator of algorithmic correctness or experimental rigor. Peer review should primarily be
performed by knowledgeable humans; generative AI can support this process, but relying on it for critical
judgement risks accepting confident yet shallow or incorrect feedback and can weaken long-term critical thinking
skills.

\subsection{GenAI critical reflection}

Generative AI was mainly useful as a productivity tool, but its output still required careful verification and
domain judgement.

\paragraph{Activities where genAI added value.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Writing support:} restructuring rough notes into clearer, more concise report-ready text.
  \item \textbf{Implementation assistance:} drafting boilerplate code for standard operators/utility functions, followed by manual verification and debugging.
  \item \textbf{Idea generation:} suggesting alternative operators or design directions when stuck, serving as a starting point for further investigation.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Activities where genAI added little or no value.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Deep algorithm design decisions:} complex EA behavior is highly context-dependent, and suggestions were often generic or misaligned with the actual failure mode.
  \item \textbf{Correctness reasoning:} outputs can sound confident while being subtly wrong, requiring thorough checking to avoid introducing bugs or invalid assumptions.
  \item \textbf{Scientific judgement:} interpreting results, identifying true bottlenecks, and deciding what to change remained primarily a human task.
\end{enumerate}

Without genAI, the final solution would likely not be fundamentally different in terms of the chosen algorithmic
components, but producing a polished deliverable (clean report text and implementation scaffolding) would have
been slower. The main impact was on speed and iteration: genAI helped draft report sections, generate initial
code versions for standard operators, and propose directions to explore when stuck. The time gain came primarily
from reducing repetitive work (rewriting, boilerplate coding, and quick idea generation), while the remaining
effort still required manual debugging, validation, and experimental evaluation to ensure correctness and
performance. Using genAI effectively is also a skill in itself: good results required precise prompting,
providing sufficient context, and critically filtering and verifying the output rather than accepting it
verbatim.
