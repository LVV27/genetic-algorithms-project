\subsection{Representation}\label{sec_rep}

\ReplaceMe{How do you represent the candidate solutions? What is your motivation to choose this one? What other options did you consider? How did you implement this specifically in Python (e.g., a list, set, numpy array, etc)?}

Candidate solutions are represented as a \textbf{permutation} of the city indices, i.e.\ a tour
$\pi = (\pi_1,\dots,\pi_n)$ that visits each city exactly once. The objective value is the total length of the
Hamiltonian cycle induced by this permutation (including the return edge to the first city).

This representation was chosen because it is the \textbf{natural encoding} for the TSP, guarantees feasibility
(no duplicated/missing cities) when using permutation-aware operators (e.g.\ OX crossover and inversion-based
mutations), and allows efficient evaluation and local search (2-opt acts directly on segments of the permutation).
Alternatives considered were an \textbf{adjacency / successor representation} ($\text{succ}[i]=j$) and
\textbf{edge-set encodings}; these can simplify overlap/diversity calculations but make crossover and repair
more involved.

In Python, each tour is stored as a \texttt{numpy.ndarray} of integers (attribute \texttt{Individual.tour}),
which enables fast slicing/reversal (used by inversion mutation and 2-opt) and supports Numba-accelerated fitness
evaluation. The deterministic crowding diversity measure additionally builds a temporary successor array
\texttt{succ} from the permutation for fast directed edge-overlap computation.


\subsection{Initialization}

\ReplaceMe{How do you initialize the population? How did you determine the number of individuals? Did you implement advanced initialization mechanisms (local search operators, heuristic solutions)? If so, describe them. Do you believe your approach maintains sufficient diversity? How do you ensure that your population enrichment scheme does not immediately take over the population? The initial population is created using a \textbf{mixed strategy}: a small fraction
($\texttt{GREEDY\_FRACTION}\cdot \lambda$) is generated with a \textbf{nearest-neighbour greedy}
heuristic (multiple random start cities), and the remaining individuals are \textbf{uniformly random
permutations}. Candidates are evaluated immediately; with \texttt{USE\_PENALTY\_FOR\_INF} enabled, invalid tours receive a large finite penalty (\texttt{INF\_PENALTY\_FACTOR}=10.0) instead of being discarded.

The population size is set to $\lambda=100$ as a trade-off under the fixed time budget: larger populations reduce the number of generations (and thus crossover opportunities), while smaller populations lose diversity and converge faster. The greedy component is intentionally kept small (default $\texttt{GREEDY\_FRACTION}=0.01$, i.e., 1 greedy individual for $\lambda=100$) to inject high-quality building blocks without dominating the population; over successive generations, crossover propagates useful edges and the population can approach greedy-like tours even from predominantly random initialization.

\subsection{Selection operators}

\ReplaceMe{Which selection operators did you implement? If they are not from the slides, describe them. Can you motivate why you chose this one? Are there parameters that need to be chosen? Did you use an advanced scheme to vary these parameters throughout the iterations? Did you try other selection operators not included in the final version? Why did you discard them?}

Parent selection uses \textbf{$k$-tournament selection}: for each parent, $k$ individuals are sampled uniformly at random from the current population and the individual with the lowest fitness is selected. This operator is simple, efficient, and offers a direct control over the trade-off between selection pressure and diversity.

The main parameter is the tournament size $\texttt{TOURNAMENT\_K}=7$. Increasing $\texttt{TOURNAMENT\_K}$ raises selection pressure, promoting early exploitation by selecting high-quality individuals (including the greedy seed) more frequently as parents, which typically yields stronger offspring. However, too large values can reduce diversity and lead to premature convergence; smaller values preserve diversity but slow progress. In the final version, $\texttt{TOURNAMENT\_K}$ is kept constant (no dynamic schedule).

\subsection{Mutation operators}

\ReplaceMe{Which mutation operators did you implement? If they are not from the slides, describe them. How do you choose among several mutation operators? Do you believe it will introduce sufficient randomness? Can that be controlled with parameters? Do you use self-adaptivity? Do you use any other advanced parameter control mechanisms (e.g., variable across iterations)? Did you try other mutation operators not included in the final version? Why did you discard them?}

A \textbf{mutation portfolio} is used for permutation tours: \texttt{swap} (exchange two cities), \texttt{inversion}
(reverse a segment), \texttt{insertion} (move one city), and \texttt{scramble} (randomly permute a segment). When
mutation is applied, the operator is sampled by a weighted roulette with probabilities 0.20, 0.60, 0.15, and
0.05 respectively.

Randomness is controlled by an \textbf{individual-specific mutation rate} $\alpha$: mutation is applied with probability $\alpha$, with $\alpha \sim U[\alpha_{\min},\alpha_{\max}]$ at initialization and inherited by offspring (self-adaptive). No generation-dependent schedule is used.

Compared to single-operator mutation (e.g.\ swap-only), the portfolio maintains higher population-level exploration; while mutation rarely improves the elite directly, it helps sustain diversity and introduces novel building blocks for crossover.


\subsection{Recombination operators}

\ReplaceMe{Which recombination operators did you implement? If they are not from the slides, describe them. How do you choose among several recombination operators? Why did you choose these ones specifically? Explain how you believe that these operators can produce offspring that combine the best features from their parents. How does your operator behave if there is little overlap between the parents? Can your recombination be controlled with parameters; what behavior do they change? Do you use self-adaptivity? Recombination uses \textbf{Order Crossover (OX)} for permutation tours. OX copies a random contiguous segment
from parent 1 into the offspring and fills the remaining positions by scanning parent 2 in order, inserting the
cities that are not yet present. This preserves a block (subtour fragment) from parent 1 while maintaining
relative order information from parent 2. Since each city is inserted at most once, OX produces a valid
\emph{permutation}; the resulting tour is therefore structurally valid, although its objective value can still be
poor.

Crossover is applied with probability $\texttt{CROSSOVER\_PROB}=1.0$; this maximizes mixing and helps introduce
new building blocks into the population under the fixed time budget. Segment boundaries are sampled uniformly
at random, which implicitly controls disruption (shorter copied segments preserve more of parent 2's order;
longer segments preserve more of parent 1's block). No self-adaptivity or generation-dependent schedule is used.

Other permutation crossovers such as PMX were considered, but OX was retained because it is simpler, robust
(always feasible), and better preserves meaningful ordering information for TSP tours in this implementation.

Survivor selection uses \textbf{deterministic crowding}. After offspring are generated, each child competes only
against its \emph{most similar} individual in the current population (its rival) and replaces it only if it has
better fitness. Similarity is measured by \textbf{directed edge overlap} using a successor representation
($\mathrm{succ}[i]$ = next city), i.e., the number of matching directed edges between the two tours; ties are
broken using closest fitness.

This local competition preserves diversity by preventing genetically distant individuals from directly replacing
each other, so genetic material is less likely to be discarded by global selection. A direct consequence is that
the population mean can remain much worse than the elite solution, since improvements mainly replace similar
tours rather than pushing the entire population toward the best.

Standard \textbf{($\mu+\lambda$) truncation} (selecting the best individuals from parents+offspring) was tested
earlier, but it increased selection pressure too strongly and caused rapid population collapse, so deterministic
crowding was retained in the final version.

\subsection{Local search operators}

\ReplaceMe{What local search operators did you implement? Describe them. Did they cause a significant improvement in the performance of your algorithm? Why (not)? Did you consider other local search operators that did not make the cut? Why did you discard them? Are there parameters that need to be determined in your operator? Do you use an advanced scheme to determine them (e.g., adaptive or self-adaptive)?}

A \textbf{Or-opt} local search is used. Or-opt removes a short consecutive chain of cities and reinserts it
elsewhere; improving moves are accepted and repeated for a limited number of iterations. The chain length is
fixed to $\ell \leq \texttt{OR\_OPT\_MAX\_CHAIN\_LEN}=3$, which provides a good trade-off between move
expressiveness and cost.

Or-opt is computationally demanding, so it is accelerated with \textbf{Numba} and applied sparingly: once
immediately after initialization, and then every $\texttt{LS\_INTERVAL}=100$ generations to only the top
$\texttt{LS\_TOP\_K}=1$ individual(s). Besides improving final quality, it typically accelerates progress by
escaping local minima via targeted reinsertions that crossover and mutation would reach only slowly through many
small steps. This component is the main driver behind consistently beating the greedy heuristic by a large
margin: without Or-opt, improvements over the heuristic were still common on dense instances (e.g.\ 50--500
cities), but on sparse instances (750--1000 cities) the GA often struggled to surpass the heuristic and only
occasionally found improvements.

\subsection{Diversity promotion mechanisms}

\ReplaceMe{Did you implement a diversity promotion scheme? If yes, which one? If no, why not? Describe the mechanism you implemented. In what sense does the mechanism improve the performance of your evolutionary algorithm? Are there parameters that need to be determined? Did you use an advanced scheme to determine them?}

Diversity is primarily maintained by the \textbf{deterministic crowding} survivor selection described in the
previous subsection. In practice, the diversity mechanism relies on computing tour similarity via the successor
representation (\texttt{build\_successor}) and selecting rivals by directed edge overlap
(\texttt{best\_rival\_index\_by\_edge\_overlap}), which is computationally demanding but helps reduce premature
convergence by limiting competition to genetically similar individuals.

A practical consequence is that the elite solution often converges quickly, while the rest of the population may
close the gap only later (and sometimes after local search has already produced a strong best solution). No
additional diversity control or adaptive scheme is used beyond this crowding-based competition.

\paragraph{Instance-dependent parameters.}
The only instance-dependent quantities are derived from the number of cities $n$ in the input distance matrix.
Each individual is a permutation of length $n$, and the sparsity detector computes the fraction of off-diagonal
$\infty$ entries over $n(n-1)$. The edge-diversity monitor depends on $n$ (and population size $|\mathcal{P}|$)
and normalizes the number of unique directed edges by
\[
\frac{\lvert E_{\text{unique}}\rvert}{\min(|\mathcal{P}|\,n,\; n(n-1))},
\]
since the number of observed unique edges cannot exceed the total edges present in the population
($|\mathcal{P}|\,n$) nor the total number of possible directed edges ($n(n-1)$).

\subsection{Strengths and weaknesses}
\ReplaceMe{What are the three main strengths of evolutionary algorithms in your experience?}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Initialization}

  Even a very small greedy seed (e.g., one individual) provides high-quality building blocks early, accelerating convergence toward short tours (including in ablations where local search is disabled); if a cheap greedy heuristic is available, it is worth using to avoid starting purely blind/random.

  \item \textbf{Diversity}

  Maintaining a population enables exploration of multiple regions of the search space in parallel; with edge-based deterministic crowding, genetic material is preserved and the population does not collapse into a single lineage.

  \item \textbf{Local search}

  Combining evolutionary search with Or-opt provides strong intensification near high-quality regions, allowing faster escape from local minima and substantial refinement once the search is close to a good basin.

\end{enumerate}

\ReplaceMe{What are the three main weak points of evolutionary algorithms in your experience?}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Sensitivity on sparse instances.}
  On sparse graphs (e.g., \texttt{tour1000}), improvements become rare and beating the greedy baseline reliably often requires strong local search (Or-opt), indicating a tendency to rely on intensification when the feasible search space is constrained.

  \item \textbf{Computational overhead of diversity control.}
  Edge-based deterministic crowding requires expensive similarity computations; Numba mitigates this, but a lighter diversity mechanism could reduce overhead and free budget for more generations/evaluations.

  \item \textbf{Stagnation and basin trapping.}
  Once a strong basin is found, the best solution can plateau for long periods, and escaping to a better optimum becomes unlikely without additional mechanisms.
\end{enumerate}

\ReplaceMe{Describe the main lessons learned from this project. Do you believe evolutionary algorithms are appropriate for the problem studied in this project? Why (not)? What surprised you and why? What did you learn from this project?}

In practice, it can be difficult to attribute improvements to specific components (selection pressure, crossover,
mutation, local search, or elimination), since multiple mechanisms interact simultaneously. Detailed logging and
controlled experiments where one component is disabled at a time are therefore important to understand where
progress actually comes from. It is also easy to focus exclusively on the best individual; however, as this is
primarily a genetic-algorithm project, the population dynamics (diversity, mean fitness, and convergence
behavior) are an essential part of the evaluation.

Another challenge is parameter sensitivity: performance can change noticeably with small parameter adjustments
and depends on instance characteristics such as tour length and sparsity. Consequently, parameter choices should
be validated across multiple instance sizes, not only on the easiest cases.

Overall, evolutionary search is an appropriate approach for quickly producing solutions that improve upon cheap
heuristics (especially on dense graphs), while exact optimality is better targeted with specialized solvers.
Genetic algorithms remain valuable when strong problem-specific solvers are unavailable, when robustness across
instances matters, or when good solutions are needed under a strict time budget.
